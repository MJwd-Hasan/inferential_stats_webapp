01 — WHO ARE WE TALKING ABOUT?

Population vs. Sample

The population is the complete group we care about. The sample is the smaller subgroup we actually observe.

We measure a few → to say something about many.

orange dots = the sample
02 — KEEP THE LANGUAGE STRAIGHT

Parameters live in populations.
Statistics live in samples.

μ / p

Parameter

A fixed, usually unknown number describing the whole population.

x̄ / p̂

Statistic

A known number calculated from sample data, used to estimate the parameter.

Population ↔ Parameter · Sample ↔ Statistic
03 — MAKE A BEST GUESS

Point Estimation

A point estimate is one number from the sample used as our best guess for an unknown population parameter.

Imagine 50 sampled apples average 150 g. Then 150 g is our estimate of the true average weight of every apple in the orchard.

Useful? Yes.
Perfect? Rarely.

x̄ = 150gour point estimate
04 — ADMIT THE UNCERTAINTY

Confidence Intervals

One estimate is a dot. A confidence interval gives a range of plausible values for the population parameter: point estimate ± margin of error.

95% confidence is about the long-run method.

If we repeatedly sampled and built intervals the same way, about 95% of those intervals would contain the true population average.

05 — PUT A CLAIM ON TRIAL

Hypothesis Testing

Hypothesis testing compares two competing claims about a population using evidence from the sample.

H₀

Null hypothesis

The default or status quo: usually “no effect,” “no difference,” or “no change.”

VS
H₁

Alternative

The claim we are trying to find evidence for.

Now ask: would our data look weird if H₀ were true?

06 — MEASURE HOW SURPRISING THE DATA ARE

The p-value

The p-value is the probability of seeing our sample data — or something more extreme — if the null hypothesis were entirely true.

shaded tail
= “this extreme
or more”
small p

Typically below 0.05 → the data are unusual under H₀ → reject H₀ in favor of the alternative.

large p

Not enough evidence to reject H₀ → keep the status quo for now.
