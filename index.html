03 — beyond the supplied guide

Imagine repeating the sample again and again.

Every random sample gives a slightly different statistic. The distribution of that statistic across repeated samples is its sampling distribution.

This idea explains why standard errors, confidence intervals, and hypothesis tests work.

Samples vary. Statistics vary. Their variation is measurable.
each sample produces one x̄
04 — point estimate + standard error

A best guess needs a measure of precision.

A point estimate is one sample-based guess for an unknown parameter. The standard error measures how much that estimator would vary from sample to sample.

For a sample mean, a common estimate is SE(x̄) = s / √n.

Larger n → smaller standard error → more precise estimates.

05 — confidence intervals

Turn one estimate into a plausible range.

A confidence interval combines a point estimate with a margin of error. In repeated sampling, a 95% procedure is designed so that about 95% of the intervals it creates contain the true parameter.

The supplied guide illustrates 150 g with an interval from 145 g to 155 g.

07 — p-values

How surprising is the sample under H₀?

The p-value is the probability of obtaining the observed result, or something more extreme, assuming the null hypothesis is true.

A small p-value means the observed result is difficult to reconcile with H₀. It is not the probability that H₀ itself is true.

08 — type I, type II, and power

Every decision rule can make mistakes.

A Type I error rejects a true H₀. A Type II error fails to reject a false H₀. Statistical power is the probability of correctly rejecting H₀ when a real effect exists.

Power generally increases with larger effects, larger samples, lower variability, and—holding other things fixed—a less stringent significance threshold.

09 — likelihood and maximum likelihood

Which parameter value makes the observed data most plausible?

The likelihood function treats the observed data as fixed and compares how strongly different parameter values are supported by those data.

The maximum likelihood estimate (MLE) is the parameter value where the likelihood is highest.

Estimate by finding the peak of support.
MLE = peak likelihood
10 — another inferential framework

Bayesian inference updates uncertainty directly.

A Bayesian analysis combines a prior distribution with the likelihood from the observed data to produce a posterior distribution.

Frequentist and Bayesian inference answer uncertainty questions differently; neither is simply “confidence intervals versus probability.” They are distinct frameworks with different interpretations.
