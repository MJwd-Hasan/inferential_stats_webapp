03 — beyond the supplied guide

Imagine repeating the sample again and again.

Every random sample gives a slightly different statistic. The distribution of that statistic across repeated samples is its sampling distribution.

This idea explains why standard errors, confidence intervals, and hypothesis tests work.

Samples vary. Statistics vary. Their variation is measurable.
each sample produces one x̄
04 — point estimate + standard error

A best guess needs a measure of precision.

A point estimate is one sample-based guess for an unknown parameter. The standard error measures how much that estimator would vary from sample to sample.

For a sample mean, a common estimate is SE(x̄) = s / √n.

Larger n → smaller standard error → more precise estimates.

05 — confidence intervals

Turn one estimate into a plausible range.

A confidence interval combines a point estimate with a margin of error. In repeated sampling, a 95% procedure is designed so that about 95% of the intervals it creates contain the true parameter.

The supplied guide illustrates 150 g with an interval from 145 g to 155 g.

07 — p-values

How surprising is the sample under H₀?

The p-value is the probability of obtaining the observed result, or something more extreme, assuming the null hypothesis is true.

A small p-value means the observed result is difficult to reconcile with H₀. It is not the probability that H₀ itself is true.

08 — type I, type II, and power

Every decision rule can make mistakes.

A Type I error rejects a true H₀. A Type II error fails to reject a false H₀. Statistical power is the probability of correctly rejecting H₀ when a real effect exists.

Power generally increases with larger effects, larger samples, lower variability, and—holding other things fixed—a less stringent significance threshold.

Reality
Correct

Do not reject a true H₀.

Type I error

Reject a true H₀. Probability = α.

Type II error

Fail to reject a false H₀. Probability = β.

Power

Reject a false H₀. Probability = 1 − β.

09 — likelihood and maximum likelihood

Which parameter value makes the observed data most plausible?

The likelihood function treats the observed data as fixed and compares how strongly different parameter values are supported by those data.

The maximum likelihood estimate (MLE) is the parameter value where the likelihood is highest.

Estimate by finding the peak of support.
MLE = peak likelihood
10 — another inferential framework

Bayesian inference updates uncertainty directly.

A Bayesian analysis combines a prior distribution with the likelihood from the observed data to produce a posterior distribution.

Frequentist and Bayesian inference answer uncertainty questions differently; neither is simply “confidence intervals versus probability.” They are distinct frameworks with different interpretations.

11 — law of large numbers & central limit theorem

Why do averages stabilize and become approximately normal?

The Law of Large Numbers says sample averages tend toward the population mean as sample size grows. The Central Limit Theorem says that, under broad conditions, the standardized sampling distribution of a sum or mean becomes approximately normal as n increases.

LLN explains convergence. CLT explains an approximate distribution for fluctuations around the target.

LLN

x̄ → μ as n grows

CLT

√n(x̄ − μ) / σ approaches a standard normal form

Why it matters

Normal-based intervals and tests often rely on this approximation

Caution

Heavy tails, dependence, or small n can make convergence slow

Large samples do not make biased designs representative; asymptotics cannot repair bad sampling.
12 — properties of estimators

Not all estimators are equally good.

We compare estimators by bias, variance, mean squared error, consistency, and efficiency. An estimator can trade a little bias for much lower variance and still have lower overall MSE.

MSE(θ̂) = Var(θ̂) + Bias(θ̂)².

Bias

E(θ̂) − θ

Variance

Consistency

Efficiency

More information with less sampling variance

Good estimation is a bias–variance–information problem, not simply “unbiased = best.”
13 — sufficiency

Can a statistic retain all sample information about a parameter?

A statistic is sufficient for a parameter when, once that statistic is known, the remaining details of the sample contain no additional information about that parameter within the assumed model.

The Fisher–Neyman factorization theorem gives a practical route for recognizing sufficiency in many parametric models.

Full data

x₁, x₂, …, xₙ

Compress

Sufficient statistic

Example idea

For Bernoulli data, the number of successes summarizes p

Sufficiency is model-dependent: the same statistic may be sufficient in one model and not another.
14 — t, chi-square, and F distributions

Different unknown quantities create different reference distributions.

When population variance is unknown, standardized means often lead to the t distribution. Variance inference for normal data uses chi-square. Ratios of scaled variances lead to the F distribution.

These are exact finite-sample results under specific assumptions, especially normality and independence.

t

Means with estimated σ

Degrees of freedom

Reference distributions encode uncertainty created by estimating unknown quantities.
15 — pivotal quantities

Build inference from a quantity whose distribution does not depend on the unknown parameter.

A pivot is a function of data and parameters with a known distribution that is free of unknown nuisance quantities. Pivots can be inverted to construct exact confidence intervals and tests.

The classic z and t statistics are examples of pivotal or approximately pivotal constructions.

16 — likelihood ratio, Wald, and score tests

Three major large-sample routes to hypothesis testing.

The likelihood ratio test compares the best fit under H₀ with the best unrestricted fit. The Wald test measures how far an estimate lies from the null in standard-error units. The score test asks how strongly the likelihood wants to move away from H₀.

Under regular conditions, these tests are asymptotically equivalent but can differ noticeably in small samples.

17 — Fisher information & Cramér–Rao

How much information about θ is contained in the sample?

Fisher information measures how sharply the likelihood changes around the true parameter. More curvature means the data distinguish nearby parameter values more strongly. The Cramér–Rao bound gives a lower variance bound for unbiased estimators under regularity conditions.

Independent observations often contribute information additively.

Flat likelihood

Sharp likelihood

more information, tighter uncertainty

Information connects likelihood curvature, estimator precision, and asymptotic standard errors.
18 — bootstrap inference

When formulas are hard, resample the observed data.

The bootstrap repeatedly samples with replacement from the observed dataset, recalculating the statistic each time. The resulting empirical distribution approximates the estimator’s sampling distribution.

Bootstrap standard errors, percentile intervals, basic intervals, and BCa intervals are common variants.

Observed sample

n observations

Resample

Recalculate

Approximate uncertainty

use the distribution of θ̂*

Bootstrap works best when the empirical sample is a reasonable stand-in for the population-generating process.
19 — permutation and randomization tests

What would happen if group labels were exchangeable under H₀?

A permutation test builds a null distribution by repeatedly rearranging labels or assignments in ways justified by the null hypothesis and the study design.

In randomized experiments, randomization inference can be especially compelling because it follows the actual assignment mechanism.

Observed statistic

p-value

Permutation validity depends on exchangeability or a justified randomization mechanism.
20 — rank-based and nonparametric inference

Inference does not always need a fully specified parametric distribution.

Rank-based procedures such as the Wilcoxon, Mann–Whitney, and Kruskal–Wallis families reduce sensitivity to distributional shape by working with order or ranks rather than raw values.

“Nonparametric” does not mean assumption-free; independence, exchangeability, symmetry, or equal-shape assumptions can still matter.

Ranks

Robustness

Trade-off

Use carefully

Choose the method for the estimand and design—not merely because a normality test rejected.
21 — multiple comparisons

More tests create more opportunities for false discoveries.

Testing many hypotheses inflates the chance of at least one false positive. Bonferroni and related methods control the family-wise error rate, while procedures such as Benjamini–Hochberg control the false discovery rate.

Multiplicity arises in many outcomes, subgroup searches, feature screening, repeated looks, and post-hoc comparisons.

FWER

Bonferroni

FDR

BH procedure

The inferential unit is often the whole family of tests, not each p-value in isolation.
22 — effect sizes & practical significance

Statistical significance is not the same as importance.

An effect can be statistically detectable yet practically tiny. Report effect sizes and uncertainty: mean differences, standardized differences, risk ratios, odds ratios, correlations, or domain-specific measures.

With huge samples, very small effects can produce tiny p-values.

p-value

Confidence interval

Context

Ask: How large is the effect? How uncertain is it? Is that magnitude meaningful?
23 — ANOVA and variance decomposition

Compare several group means through explained and unexplained variation.

ANOVA partitions total variability into variation between groups and variation within groups. Their scaled ratio produces an F statistic.

A significant omnibus F test says at least one mean differs; post-hoc comparisons are needed to locate differences while accounting for multiplicity.

Total variation

Between groups

Within groups

residual variation inside groups

F ratio

ANOVA is a linear-model comparison: does adding group indicators explain enough variation?
24 — regression inference

Estimate conditional relationships while quantifying uncertainty.

In linear regression, coefficients describe changes in the conditional mean associated with predictors, given the model. Standard errors support coefficient intervals and tests; the model F test evaluates groups of coefficients.

Inference depends on assumptions about specification, independence, variance, and the meaning of conditioning variables.

Association in a regression coefficient is not automatically a causal effect.
25 — categorical-data inference

Counts and proportions have their own inferential toolkit.

For contingency tables, chi-square procedures compare observed counts with counts expected under a null model. Exact procedures such as Fisher’s exact test are useful in small sparse tables.

For binary outcomes, risk differences, risk ratios, and odds ratios often communicate more than a test alone.

A small p-value signals dependence; effect measures describe how large that dependence is.
26 — assumptions and diagnostics

Inference is only as trustworthy as the model-data relationship.

Diagnostics check whether assumptions behind standard errors, intervals, and tests are plausible. Important issues include independence, linearity, variance structure, distributional shape, leverage, and influential observations.

A diagnostic is evidence about model adequacy, not a ritual checklist.

Residuals

Leverage

Influence

Specification

When assumptions fail, change the model or the inferential method—not merely the plot.
27 — robust and sandwich standard errors

Sometimes keep the coefficient model but relax the variance model.

Heteroskedasticity-consistent or sandwich standard errors can provide asymptotically valid uncertainty estimates when the conditional variance is misspecified, under appropriate independence and regularity conditions.

Cluster-robust standard errors extend this idea when observations are dependent within groups.

Robust SEs repair some variance assumptions; they do not fix bias from omitted variables or bad identification.
28 — credible intervals & posterior predictive inference

Bayesian uncertainty can be summarized directly from the posterior.

A credible interval contains a chosen posterior probability for a parameter under the specified model and prior. Posterior predictive distributions integrate over parameter uncertainty to predict future or replicated data.

Posterior predictive checks compare replicated data from the fitted model with patterns in the observed data.

Bayesian probability statements are conditional on the model, prior, and observed data.
29 — Bayes factors and model comparison

Compare how well competing models predict the observed data before seeing it.

A Bayes factor compares marginal likelihoods of two models. It updates prior odds between models into posterior odds.

Bayes factors can be sensitive to prior choices because they integrate likelihood over each model’s parameter space.

Prior odds

Bayes factor

Posterior odds

Sensitivity

30 — statistical decision theory

Inference can end with an action, not only an estimate.

Decision theory combines uncertain states of nature with a loss function. A decision rule is judged by its expected loss or risk, making explicit how different mistakes are valued.

The “best” statistical decision can change when the cost of false positives and false negatives changes.

31 — sequential inference & optional stopping

Looking at the data repeatedly changes the inferential problem.

If researchers repeatedly test as data accumulate and stop when p < 0.05, the nominal Type I error rate can inflate. Group-sequential, alpha-spending, and related methods adjust inference for planned interim looks.

Always distinguish a fixed-sample analysis from a design that allows data-dependent stopping.

Interim looks

inspect results before final n

Naive stopping

Sequential design

Alpha spending

The sampling plan is part of the inferential model.
32 — statistical vs. causal inference

A strong association is not automatically a causal effect.

Causal inference requires assumptions about how treatment or exposure relates to potential outcomes. Randomization, adjustment for confounders, matching, weighting, instruments, and natural experiments address different identification problems.

Statistical inference quantifies uncertainty given an estimand and design; causal inference additionally asks whether the estimand has a causal interpretation.

Association

P(Y|X) relationship

Causal estimand

contrast of potential outcomes

Identification

connect causal quantity to observed data

Estimation

quantify effect and uncertainty

No amount of standard-error precision can rescue an unidentified causal question.
33 — missing data and inferential validity

Missingness can change both precision and bias.

Under MCAR, missingness is unrelated to observed and unobserved values. Under MAR, missingness may depend on observed data. MNAR allows dependence on unobserved values even after conditioning on observed information.

Complete-case analysis, multiple imputation, weighting, and sensitivity analysis rely on different assumptions.

MCAR

missingness unrelated to data

MAR

explained by observed information

MNAR

still depends on unobserved values

Sensitivity

ask how conclusions change under alternatives

Handling missing data is an inferential assumption problem, not merely a data-cleaning step.
34 — meta-analysis

Combine evidence across studies while respecting heterogeneity.

A meta-analysis pools study-level effect estimates. Fixed-effect models target a common effect under strong homogeneity assumptions; random-effects models allow true effects to vary across studies.

Between-study heterogeneity, publication bias, study quality, and dependence can dominate the pooled estimate.

Study effects
